Spatiotemporal GNNs for Real-Time Fault
Localization in Power Grids
Anik Kumar Basu, Sabrina Shonda Snaha, Md. Sadik Mahmud Adive
Department of Computer Science and Engineering
Bangladesh University of Business and Technology, Bangladesh
Abstract—Fast fault identification and bus-level localization are
essential for robust power grids under renewable integration and
quickly changing operational circumstances. Many conventional
relays and topology-agnostic classifiers struggle to capture how
disturbances propagate through the network. This work presents
aSpatiotemporal Graph Neural Network(ST-GNN) that combines
spatial message passing on the grid graph with temporal
sequence modeling of PMU windows. The model uses two Graph
Convolutional Network (GCN) layers followed by a two-layer Long
Short-Term Memory (LSTM) module, trained with a multi-task
objective for fault-bus localization and fault-type classification.
Experiments on the IEEE 39-bus New England system with
a synthetic dataset of approximately 60 000 expanded samples
summarized in the dataset statistics figure achieve 92.8% top-1
localization accuracy, 97.4% fault detection accuracy, and 1.3
mean absolute bus-index error. The reported results show that the
proposed model comfortably meets the project targets, remains
competitive with the learned baselines, and clearly benefits from
using both spatial (GCN) and temporal (LSTM) components
together.
Index Terms—Graph Neural Networks, Smart Grid, Fault
Localization, Spatiotemporal Learning, LSTM, GCN, Power

I. INTRODUCTION
Modern transmission grids are increasingly dynamic due to
renewable integration, bidirectional power flows, and high-rate
sensing (e.g., PMUs) [5]. Faults can propagate quickly through
electrically coupled regions, so the operational goal extends
beyond detection to identifying the originating bus with low
latency.
Impedance- and threshold-based relays remain useful but
can degrade when parameters shift or transients are complex
[7]. Topology-agnostic learning (e.g., CNNs on ordered bus
vectors) can perform well in fixed settings but is not naturally
permutation-consistent with the grid graph [6]. GNNs match
the topology by propagating along edges [12], but spatial-only
models do not explicitly capture the transient evolution that
distinguishes fault types and severity [11].
We propose aSpatiotemporal Graph Neural Network
(ST-GNN)that couples GCN-based spatial aggregation with
LSTM-based temporal modeling over sliding PMU windows.
The main contributions are:
• A hybrid GCN + LSTM architecture that jointly models
graph topology and temporal fault dynamics on the IEEE
39-bus New England system.
• An expanded synthetic dataset of approximately 60 000
labeled samples generated from raw IEEE 39-bus fault
simulations and stochastic transient replay, covering four
fault types under multiple resistance and load settings.
• A multi-task training objective that simultaneously op-
timizes fault-bus localization (primary) and fault-type
classification (auxiliary), improving generalization of the
shared backbone.
• Ablation and robustness studies (noise and N-1 contingen-
cies) that quantify sensitivity to measurement noise and
topology perturbations.
II. RELATEDWORK
A. Traditional and Shallow Learning Methods
Traditional fault-detection pipelines relied on impedance re-
lays, threshold rules, and related signal-processing logic. These
methods are fast and interpretable, but their accuracy depends
on accurate line parameters, relay settings, and stable operating
assumptions. In renewable-rich grids, those assumptions are
less reliable because inverter behavior, topology changes, and
bidirectional power flow can alter fault signatures. Liao et
al. [5] and Chen et al. [7] both note that data-driven fault
diagnosis becomes attractive when fixed rules are no longer
flexible enough.
As PMU data became more available, shallow classifiers
such as SVMs, random forests, decision trees, and shallow
ANNs were adopted for fault-type classification and coarse
localization. These models can learn nonlinear boundaries, but
they still depend heavily on handcrafted features and often
ignore the physical topology of the grid. For bus-level fault
localization, that limitation is serious because electrically close
buses may appear unrelated in a plain feature vector.
B. Deep Learning for Fault Diagnosis
CNN-based approaches reduce feature engineering by learn-
ing directly from waveform or feature-map inputs, but they still
assume an ordered Euclidean layout and can be sensitive to
bus indexing [6]. Recurrent models, especially LSTMs, capture
temporal evolution effectively [13], which makes them useful
for fault transients. However, a pure temporal model does not
explicitly represent electrical connectivity, so it can miss the
spatial relationships needed for bus-level localization.
Recent work has also moved toward richer measurement
inputs and joint diagnostic tasks. Ngo et al. [1] combine
graph-attention layers with one-dimensional convolutions to
jointly detect faults, identify their types, and localize them
in distribution systems. Their results reinforce the value of

combining spatial structure with transient measurements, while
also highlighting the practical importance of robustness to

C. Graph Neural Networks in Power Systems
GNNs address this mismatch by learning directly on the
bus-line graph. Kipf and Welling [12] provided the graph-
convolutional foundation used in many later power-system
studies. Chen et al. [7], Donon et al. [9], and James et al.
[8] showed that topology-aware learning improves diagnosis
and localization performance in power networks. These results
suggest that the grid topology itself should be part of the model,
not just the input feature set.
More recent studies extend this direction beyond con-
ventional bus measurements. Peng et al. [3] formulate
secondary-circuit fault location in smart substations as a graph-
classification problem, demonstrating that graph representations
can also support device-level diagnosis in complex substation
networks. At the benchmark level, Varbella et al. [4] released
PowerGraph, a public collection of power-flow and cascading-
failure graph tasks that includes IEEE test systems. This
addresses an important reproducibility issue in a field where
many studies still rely on private or synthetic data.
The advantage of GNNs is physical consistency. If the graph
representation is constructed correctly, the model learns from
electrical neighborhoods instead of relying on arbitrary bus
indexing. GNNs also scale well for sparse networks, since real
power grids have far fewer lines than all possible bus-to-bus
connections. The main limitation is that many GNN-based
approaches still use static snapshots, which may not capture
enough information to distinguish the true fault location when
neighboring buses experience similar disturbances.
D. Spatiotemporal Learning and Research Gap
Spatiotemporal graph models combine graph-based spatial
aggregation with temporal sequence modeling. Yu et al. [11]
demonstrated this idea in sequence prediction by learning both
network structure and temporal dynamics. For fault localization,
the same principle is especially relevant because the most
discriminative evidence often appears in the first few cycles
after fault inception.
Still, many existing studies remain snapshot-based or focus
on classification rather than bus-level localization. Robustness
under measurement noise, topology changes, and class im-
balance is also reported inconsistently. These gaps motivate
the ST-GNN design used in this paper, which combines
graph message passing with temporal modeling for joint fault
location and fault-type prediction. Recent uncertainty-aware
graph learning also identifies long-tailed fault distributions and
confidence estimation as important deployment concerns [2];
these considerations motivate the class-balancing and noise-
robustness experiments in this work.

C. Problem Statement
Given a sliding window of T= 50 consecutive timestep
observations—representing 500 ms at a 100 Hz PMU sampling
rate—expressed as a three-dimensional tensor Xt ∈R 39×5×50,
the ST-GNN must simultaneously predict:
1) Fault localization label ˆyloc ∈ {0,1, . . . ,39}: classes 0–
38 denote the faulted bus, while class 39 denotes normal
operation.
2) Fault type label ˆytype ∈ {SLG,LL,DLG,3PH,Normal}:
the fault category or healthy state.
The two tasks share all learnable representations through the
end of the pooling layer, enabling the model to benefit from
auxiliary supervision even when only one label is of primary
operational interest.
IV. PROPOSEDMETHODOLOGY
A. Overall Methodology Pipeline
As illustrated in Fig. 3, the workflow consists of four stages:
(i) defining the grid graph and features, (ii) simulating faults
and extracting windows, (iii) training the ST-GNN with a multi-
task objective, and (iv) producing predictions for fault location
and type.
We generate labeled fault transients via simulation, stan-
dardize node features using training-set statistics, and train the
ST-GNN end-to-end with a multi-task loss for localization and
fault type.
B. Synthetic Dataset Generation
Since real-world fault event logs from utilities are confi-
dential and extremely scarce, we generate a labeled synthetic
dataset using Pandapower [10], a Python library that imple-
ments the Newton-Raphson power-flow solver and standard
IEEE short-circuit models. The IEEE 39-bus network is loaded
via pandapower.networks.case39() and faults are
injected through the pandapower.shortcircuit module.
In the implementation, the short-circuit calculation is not treated
as a full time-domain simulator; instead, it provides the fault-
severity seed that drives a custom 50-step transient generator.
Each stored sample is therefore one PMU-like tensor with
200 ms of pre-fault steady state (20 samples) followed by
300 ms of synthesized post-fault response (30 samples) at
100 Hz.

F . Multi-Task Training
The combined loss function is:
Ltotal =αL loc + (1−α)L type, α= 0.7,(5)
where both terms are weighted cross-entropy losses and α=
0.7 prioritizes localization precision—operationally the more
critical task for maintenance crews. Training uses the Adam
optimizer ( β1 = 0.9 , β2 = 0.999 ) with an initial learning
rate of 10−3, reduced on plateau (factor 0.5, patience 10),
batch size 32, weight decay 10−4, and early stopping with
patience 20 on validation loss. Each epoch iterates over mini-
batches of windowed graph tensors, runs forward passes for
both heads, and updates parameters via backpropagation on
Ltotal. Validation loss controls learning-rate scheduling and
early stopping.

C. Baseline Comparison
We compare ST-GNN against threshold heuristics, Random
Forest, linear SVM, and an MLP. The exact ranking depends on
the split, class balance, and how aggressively the conventional
baselines are tuned. In the experiment reported in Fig. 13, the
ST-GNN achieves the strongest top-1 localization accuracy
among the plotted models, while the broader contribution
of the method is its topology-aware inductive bias and its
robustness analyses under noise and topology variation. The
confusion matrices in Fig. 12 show that localization errors
often concentrate on nearby buses rather than being distributed
uniformly across the system.
D. Ablation Study and Robustness
We evaluate ablations and robustness to measurement noise
and N-1 topology changes (Fig. 14).
Ablation.Removing the LSTM while retaining the GCN
(spatial-only model) drops top-1 accuracy by 10.7 percentage
points to 82.1%, consistent with the loss of temporal transient
information. Removing the GCN while keeping an LSTM over
flattened node features (temporal-only) drops accuracy by 20.3
percentage points to 72.5%, confirming that topology encoding
is indispensable.
Noise sensitivity.The model maintains above-90% accuracy
up to σ= 0.01 p.u. (equivalent to typical PMU measurement
noise) and degrades gracefully to 85.3% atσ= 0.05p.u. and
78.6% atσ= 0.10p.u. (extreme noise).

N-1 contingency.When a transmission line is removed

proxy, but we do not claim that adjacent bus numbers always
correspond to adjacent electrical locations. A graph-hop or
impedance-distance localization metric would be a stronger
choice for future studies.
VI. CONCLUSION
We presented a Spatiotemporal Graph Neural Network for
real-time fault localization in power transmission grids, target-
ing the IEEE 39-bus New England benchmark. By combining
Graph Convolutional Networks for topological spatial encoding
with LSTM networks for temporal transient modelling—and
training both jointly under a multi-task localization-plus-
classification objective—the proposed ST-GNN achieves strong
results across a range of simulated fault conditions.
Empirical evaluations show that the joint spatiotemporal
design outperforms classical rule-based estimators and delivers
strong localization accuracy on the reported experimental
setup. More importantly, the model remains resilient under
measurement noise and N-1 topology changes, which is central
to its practical value. Future work includes attention-based
message passing and validation on field-recorded PMU events.
